Part I: Hypothesis Testing

Step 1: One-Sample T-Test for a Mean

The Mayo Clinic states that a normal resting heart rate for adults ranges from 60 to 100 beats per minute, but the national average for college-aged students is widely considered to be 72 bpm. Let's test if the average resting pulse of our Stat 2050 population is significantly different from 72 bpm.

Run a formal hypothesis test using a significance level of α = 0.05alpha equals 0.05. You must provide the following 5 steps clearly labeled in your report:

  1. Hypotheses: State the null (H0H naught) and alternative (H1H A) hypotheses using correct notation.
  2. Conditions: Briefly state if the conditions for testing are met (Randomness, Independence, Normal/Large Sample).
  3. Test Statistic: Calculate and report the t-test statistic and the degrees of freedom (df).
  4. P-Value: Report the calculated p-value.
  5. Conclusion: State your decision (Reject or Fail to Reject H0H naught) and write a concluding sentence in the context of the problem.

Step 2: Two-Sample Independent T-Test

Is there a significant difference in resting pulse rates between male and female students? Let's conduct a two-sample independent t-test to find out. Use a significance level of α = 0.05alpha equals 0.05.

  1. Hypotheses: State H0H naught and H1H A (assume we are testing for any difference, so it is a two-tailed test).
  2. Test Statistic: Calculate and report the 2-sample t-test statistic and the degrees of freedom (df).
  3. P-Value: Report the calculated p-value.
  4. Conclusion: State your decision and write a concluding sentence comparing male and female pulse rates based on your findings.

Step 3: One-Sample Proportion Z-Test

According to the CDC, roughly 50% of U.S. adults meet the guidelines for aerobic physical activity. Let's test if the proportion of our Stat 2050 class that exercises vigorously is significantly different from this 50% national benchmark. Use a significance level of α = 0.05alpha equals 0.05.

  1. Hypotheses: State the null (H0H naught) and alternative (H1H A) hypotheses using correct proportion notation (pp).
  2. Conditions: Verify the success/failure conditions (np ≥ 10n times p is greater than or equal to 10 and n(1-p) ≥ 10n times 1 minus p is greater than or equal to 10) to ensure a normal model is appropriate.
  3. Test Statistic: Calculate and report the 1-proportion z-test statistic.
  4. P-Value: Report the calculated p-value.
  5. Conclusion: State your decision and write a concluding sentence in the context of the problem.

Step 4: Chi-Square Goodness of Fit Test

The American Academy of Ophthalmology estimates that the United States distribution of eye color is approximately: 45% Brown, 27% Blue, 18% Hazel, and 10% Green/Other. Let's run a Goodness of Fit test to determine if our Stat 2050 class follows this national distribution. Use a significance level of α = 0.05alpha equals 0.05.

  1. Hypotheses: State the null (H0H naught) and alternative (H1H A) hypotheses in words.
  2. Expected Counts Table: Using the total sample size from your class survey, calculate the expected number of students for each of the four eye color categories. Create and include a table in your report that displays both the Observed Counts and your calculated Expected Counts for each category. (Check: Are all expected counts ≥ 5?)
  3. Test Statistic: Calculate and report the χ2Chi-squared test statistic and the degrees of freedom (df).
  4. P-Value: Report the calculated p-value.
  5. Conclusion: State your decision and write a concluding sentence in the context of our class eye color distribution.
  6. The Personal Connection: State your actual eye color. Calculate the individual Chi-Square contribution for your specific eye color category using the formula (O - E)² / E(Observed minus Expected) squared, divided by Expected. Did your eye color category contribute a large or small amount to the overall test statistic? Explain what this means about how your eye color category compares to the expected national average.
