Student Instructions

Part I: Linear Regression & Outlier Analysis
⚠️ Real Data Alert: Real data is messy. You may calculate an initial correlation coefficient (rr) that is close to zero. This is a normal part of the data analysis process before exploring outliers.

Step 1: Initial Scatterplot & Analysis

  1. Variables: Use Pulse as the Explanatory Variable (xx) and Height_Inches as the Response Variable (yy).
  2. Visualize: Generate a scatterplot that includes the line of best fit. (Your plot must include a title and labeled axes).
  3. Calculate: Find the correlation coefficient (rr), the coefficient of determination (R2R-squared), and the regression equation (ŷ = ax + by-hat equals a times x plus b). Record these values in your report near your scatterplot.
    📐 Rounding Rules: You must report rr, the slope, and the y-intercept to three decimal places (the thousandths place). You must report R2R-squared to four decimal places.
  4. Analyze: Based on the visual scatterplot and your rr value, briefly explain whether a linear model appears appropriate for this raw data.

Step 2: Identifying Suspicious Points

Look closely at your scatterplot (or refer to boxplots from Phase 1) to identify any unusual data points.

  1. List the Outliers: Create a list of the suspected outliers written as ordered pairs (Pulse, Height).
  2. Evaluate: For each outlier, state whether it has high or low leverage (extreme in the x-direction) and whether it has a large or small residual (extreme in the y-direction).
  3. Hypothesize: Do you suspect these points are influential? (You will prove this in Step 3).
💻 Tech Tip: Removing Outliers without Deleting Data
If you need to remove an outlier to see how the model improves, don't delete the entire row! Find the outlier in your dataset and simply add a letter to the numerical value (e.g., change a pulse of 200 to x200). Statistical software will ignore the text entry, dropping it from the scatterplot calculations while keeping your dataset intact.

Step 3: Model Refinement & Testing Influence

  1. Refine: Remove the suspected outlier(s) using the tip above. Note: Once extreme outliers are removed, other hidden outliers may suddenly become apparent. Repeat the removal process until you feel the model is as clean as is reasonable.
  2. The Final Model: Generate a new scatterplot without the outliers. Include the new line of best fit, title, and axis labels. Recalculate rr, R2R-squared, and the regression equation for this new data set. (Remember to follow the rounding rules from Step 1).
  3. Confirm Influence: Compare your new regression equation to your original one. Did the slope or intercept change drastically? Based on this, were the points you removed truly influential points?

Step 4: Interpretation & Predictions (Using the Refined Model)

Note: Proceed with using your refined model to complete the following interpretations and predictions, even if a linear model ultimately does not seem like the right fit for these two variables.
  1. Analyze R2R-squared: Write a sentence explaining exactly what your new R2R-squared value tells you about how well the Explanatory variable accounts for the variability in the Response variable.
  2. Contextualize the Intercept: Interpret the y-intercept of your refined equation. Does this intercept make logical sense in the real world? Why or why not? (Hint: Consider what an x-value of 0 implies).
  3. Evaluate the Causality: In this model, we are using Pulse to predict Height. Think critically about this causal direction. Does a change in your pulse *cause* a change in your height? Write a brief sentence on why choosing Height as the Explanatory variable instead might have made more logical sense.
  4. Standard Prediction: Use your refined equation to predict the height of a student who has a resting pulse of 75 bpm.
  5. Personal Prediction & Residual: Use the equation to predict your own height based on your resting pulse. Calculate your Residual (Actual - PredictedActual minus Predicted). Interpret your residual in context (e.g., "My residual is negative, so my actual height is lower than the model predicted.")
Part II: Probability & Methods

Step 5: Probability

Create a Two-Way Table (Contingency Table) summarizing the class data using the categorical variable for Vigorous Exercise (Rows) and the ordinal variable for Math Anxiety (Columns). Ensure your columns account for all rating levels (1 through 4).
Reminder: 1 = No Math Anxiety and 4 = High Math Anxiety.

Include this contingency table in your report. Then, calculate the following probabilities based on your table (show your un-simplified fractions as your work):

  • P(High Anxiety): Probability a randomly selected student has High Math Anxiety (rating of 4).
  • P(Exercises Vigorously AND Low Anxiety): Probability of the Intersection (using anxiety rating of 1).
  • P(Does Not Exercise Vigorously OR High Anxiety): Probability of the Union (Addition Rule).
  • P(High Anxiety | Exercises Vigorously): The conditional probability that a student has high anxiety, given that they exercise vigorously.
  • P(Exercises Vigorously | High Anxiety): The conditional probability that a student exercises vigorously, given that they have high math anxiety.
